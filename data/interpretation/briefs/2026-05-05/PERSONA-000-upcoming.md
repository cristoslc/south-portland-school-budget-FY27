## Since Last Meeting

**The school board adopted a budget and sent it to the City Council.**
On April 7, the board voted 4–2 to advance the FY27 superintendent's budget to the City Council. The budget totals approximately $75.8 million for K–12 (plus $250,000 for Adult Education), and is designed to keep the local tax increase below 6 percent. That 6 percent figure represents a roughly $2.6 million increase over the current year's $73.1 million K–12 budget.

**The board made two formal requests to the City Council.**
At the April 13 meeting, the board voted on what to ask of the Council at the April 14 workshop:

- **$360,000 in shared-services relief** (4–1 vote): The board asked the city to absorb the cost of School Resource Officers (estimated at roughly $220,000) and school snow removal and sanding (estimated at roughly $140,000). If granted, the board voted 3–2 to direct those savings into contingency — a financial cushion — rather than restoring positions.
- **A 1 percent additional tax increase directed entirely to contingency** (unanimous vote): Each additional 1 percent on the local tax rate adds roughly $540,000. The board wants this money held as a financial buffer rather than spent on new programs or staff. A separate motion to use a 0.5 percent increase to restore laid-off employees failed for lack of a second.

**Vice Chair Adrian Dowling resigned; Member Smith was elected Vice Chair.**
Chair DeAngelis noted Dowling's service and thanked him. The board unanimously elected Member Smith as the new Vice Chair at the April 13 meeting.

**The board announced it would formally vote to halt reconfiguration for fall 2026.**
Chair DeAngelis stated publicly on April 13 that the board has strong support for delaying elementary school reconfiguration from September 2026 to a later date (fall 2027 was referenced). A formal vote was scheduled for a special meeting on **April 29**. That meeting is not yet documented in the available sources, so its outcome is not confirmed here.

**The Finance Director said closing Kaylor Elementary remains the financially necessary path.**
Director Abigail Ketchen was asked directly at the April 13 meeting whether keeping Kaylor open was financially possible. Her answer: "I really don't think so." She said she would advocate for it if she saw a real financial path but could not recommend it. Kaylor's full allocated cost in the pre-reduction budget was estimated at roughly $5.7 million; after reductions, its closure accounts for approximately $4 million — about half of all the cuts made.

**The current fiscal year (FY26) is projected to run a deficit.**
Ketchen reported at the April 13 finance committee update that approximately 95 percent of FY26 funds are either spent or encumbered. The updated projected deficit range is **$80,000 to $1.1 million**. A separate, unquantified amount — overtime pay owed to staff under the SPTA contract for work beyond 15 hours — has not yet been calculated and will add to that deficit once submissions are collected. The city's fund balance would cover any shortfall, with a repayment arrangement still to be worked out.

**Three resignations were reported.**
Victoria Argese (special education teacher, Skillin, effective August 31), Caitlin Field (grade 1 teacher, Dyer, effective August 31), and Beth Kellogg (principal, Brown Elementary, effective June 30).

**The Finance Director offered an early look at FY28.**
Ketchen said a rough roll-forward model suggests the district can likely stay near 6 percent next year, but only with careful planning. Key unknowns include health insurance rates, state funding under the new Essential Programs and Services (EPS) formula, and whether the district can avoid further structural cuts. She declined to make a firm prediction.

## What This Meeting Is For

The **May 5 City Council public hearing** is the formal step where the Council votes on the school budget bottom line and decides what to send to voters. Under state law and the city charter, the Council can raise or lower the total school budget number — but cannot direct the school department on specific line items, positions, or building decisions.

If the Council changes the bottom-line figure, the School Board would then need to meet to decide how to absorb the cut or spend the addition. The revised number would go on the **June 9 validation referendum**, where South Portland residents vote to approve or reject it.

This is one of the most consequential steps in the budget calendar. The public hearing is the last formal opportunity for residents to comment before the Council votes on what goes to the ballot.

## What To Watch For

- **The Council's vote on the bottom-line number.** Listen for the specific dollar figure and the percentage increase it represents. If the Council raises or lowers the number, the School Board will need to reconvene before the June 9 referendum.
- **Whether the Council addresses the $360,000 shared-services request.** If they agree to absorb SRO and snow-removal costs, the school budget could show a smaller local-tax increase while the city's own budget absorbs those costs. Watch for whether Councilors treat this as a real possibility or deflect it.
- **Whether the Council signals support for a tax-rate increase above 6 percent.** The school board's unanimous vote was for 1 percent more directed to contingency. Some Councilors may push back on tax impact; others may support it. Any signal here shapes what goes to voters on June 9.
- **Public comment on Kaylor and reconfiguration.** Kaylor families and staff have been consistent and vocal. Listen for whether the Council engages with equity concerns raised about which school absorbed the closure, and whether any Councilor attempts to weigh in on building decisions (which, under state law, they legally cannot direct).
- **Any mention of the FY26 deficit and city repayment.** The school district is projected to end this year in deficit, drawing on city reserves. Watch for whether Councilors raise the repayment arrangement and what that means for next year's budget picture.
- **Whether the Council places conditions or caveats.** Councilors cannot dictate specific line items, but they can vote a higher or lower number and expect the school board to sort out the details. Listen for whether any Councilor attaches informal expectations to their vote.
- **Whether new state funding is confirmed.** The school board's April 7 presentation referenced potential additional state dollars — roughly $152,000 for lower-income students and $150,000 for homeless youth — that had not yet appeared in the official state allocation. If confirmed, they could shift the numbers slightly.
- **The referendum date and what voters will actually decide.** The June 9 validation referendum is a public vote to approve or reject the school budget the Council forwards. Listen for clear explanation of what a "yes" or "no" vote on June 9 would mean in practice.
