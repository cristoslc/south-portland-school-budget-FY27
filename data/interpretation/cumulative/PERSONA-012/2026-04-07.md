# Cumulative Record: Jaylen (PERSONA-012)
## Meeting: School Board Regular Meeting -- April 7, 2026 -- 2026-04-07

### Interpretation

The budget passed 4-2, the meeting was held in his school's lecture hall, and SPHS was not mentioned once in three hours and forty-nine minutes. That is April 7. He had said at the end of the last meeting that he knew where he was going. He went. What he found was the complete erasure of his school from a public hearing held inside it.

The piece that will sit with him longest is not the vote — he saw that coming. It is Jen Fletcher, a middle school band director, walking to the microphone and explaining what happened when teachers and families pushed back on the percussion cut across multiple meetings and multiple emails: they were told, directly or indirectly, that it would not make a difference. He does not know who communicated this or in exactly what context — Fletcher's testimony is one teacher's account of what advocates heard, and he is careful not to read more into it than it can bear. But it is the first time he has heard that framing from someone who was actually in those conversations, and it arrives the week before May 5.

He has been mapping student and community exclusion since December — the forum where Angela and Alex sat without speaking, the superintendent search that excluded students until the board reversed itself, the community member at the February 10 joint session who named the problem aloud while the council nodded and changed nothing. Every point on that map was about systems not designed for student voice. Fletcher's testimony describes something different in kind: organized advocacy told in advance it would not affect the outcome. He cannot confirm the full picture from a public comment testimony, and he distinguishes that carefully. But he also cannot unhear it.

Board member Richardson says "related arts teachers are the joy and the light of middle school" and votes no. The budget passes 4-2. That is the Walker pattern — naming the problem correctly, being in the minority — and it keeps happening in both buildings.

The public comment cap is the first thing that is new in kind. The Zoom rejection in December was a prior-experience response to a real problem. The adult-centered forum design was a habit. Unanimously voting to cap public comment at 75 minutes per session is the board writing a formal limit on community access into the rules. Feller called it "a little draconian" before voting for it.

The contingent related arts position — one year, state funding dependent, unspecified subject, subject to recall — arrives in the same wrapper as every prior provisional good news. He recognizes the shape.

Three hours and forty-nine minutes. His school. Nobody said SPHS.
