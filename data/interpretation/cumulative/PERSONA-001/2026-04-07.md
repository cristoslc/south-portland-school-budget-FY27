# Cumulative Record: Maria (PERSONA-001)
## Meeting: School Board Regular Meeting -- April 7, 2026 -- 2026-04-07

### Interpretation

Maria walks into April 7 carrying one real piece of evidence from April 2: the board removed the early release days after parents pushed back. First concrete reversal in five months. She is holding it carefully, like a match she doesn't want to waste.

And then the health insurance number lands: 5.14%, not the 11.5% placeholder. Roughly $655,000 freed. Two elementary teachers and two special education ed techs coming back, one year only under Article 18 recall. She is going to take it, and she is immediately aware of the string attached — "one-year only" means this fight restarts in spring 2027, with the same gap, the same forums, the same math. It is a patch. She knows what patches look like now. But it is also proof that the number the district cited for months as immovable moved when something changed. That matters.

What does not move — what keeps moving further in the wrong direction — is the behavioral strategist question. The position is not being recalled. It is being replaced with a board-certified behavior analyst. The administration says this is a stronger clinical model. Board member Richardson says she does not understand why, given the available recall option and the community feedback, and asks the question twice. Maria has been tracking this position since December 17, when she first saw "16 educational technicians" on a cut list and started understanding that these roles are not interchangeable. She has carried the caseload number since March 30 — nearly 60 students, more than 40 formal behavior plans. The administration is not cutting the function; it is redefining it. That is harder to fight because the district can claim equivalence. Maria is not yet certain it is wrong. She is certain she does not have enough information to know, and that this is the board's last meeting before city council.

The Dyer classroom testimony is the moment the meeting becomes something she cannot reason away. An ed tech describes a functional life skills classroom that has been short one or two staff for nearly the entire school year, safety incidents, no extra pay for covering vacancies. A teacher says she has not taken a full 45-minute lunch in 14 years because she cannot leave the room. The superintendent's response — she had not heard that safety was a deficit — lands in the space between those testimonies and the plan to replace two embedded occupational therapists with ed tech positions that have been listed and unfilled all year. Maria is not attributing bad faith to that answer. She is noting that it does not match what was said at that microphone, and that the board voted four to two to pass this budget to city council without resolving the question of what reconfiguration will actually cost.

Kate Normandin's testimony about PTA dissolution is the thing Maria has not thought through yet and is already dreading. Every PTA is its own nonprofit — its own bylaws, its own bank accounts, its own filing status. Reconfiguration means those organizations do not transfer. They have to be rebuilt. By parents who already have full-time jobs. In the same year that everything else is in flux. Maria runs a PTA. She knows exactly how long it takes to hold an officer election, write bylaws, reconcile accounts. Nobody in the administration has accounted for this cost.

The teacher survey is the data she has been sensing in her parent networks all winter finally made visible: 92% negative or very negative morale, 76% opposing the budget, 84% saying cuts should come from higher administration rather than student-facing positions. She is putting those numbers in every parent channel she runs tonight. Not because she thinks a survey changes the vote that already happened. Because April 14 is one week away, and she wants every parent in that room to know exactly what the teachers think.
