# Cumulative Record: Dana (PERSONA-009)
## Meeting: City Council Budget Workshop #2 — April 28, 2026 — 2026-04-28

### Interpretation

April 28 is the meeting Dana reads the agenda for, clocks the fund balance fight, and decides not to send a crew. The story is real — 42 teachers potentially out the door, city staff on the record recommending against the school bailout that a council majority appears ready to deliver anyway, and a tax rate that could climb from 5.1% to 6.5% if everything sitting in the parking lot gets funded. But nothing happens tonight. Everything goes into the parking lot. May 12 is the vote. Tonight is a staging session with a long warm-up act through six small departments before the school item even reaches the floor.

What makes the fund balance fight structurally different from anything Dana has tracked since December is that the math has now split three ways, and none of the parties agree on the number. City staff — the professional administration — says forgive nothing. They warn it forces either delayed capital projects or a tax increase on the city side of the ledger. The city's own conservative estimate says the school probably needs $3 million. The school department says it is actually $1 to $1.5 million. The council is being asked to name a dollar figure tonight — not a percentage — so the school can finally close its FY27 budget. Dana has been covering this as a yes/no question since February: will the council forgive debt or will they not? It has matured into a three-party bargaining process, and the school district cannot finish its books until someone blinks.

The parking lot mechanism is the city council's version of a pattern Dana knows intimately. She watched the school board run the same deferral play across four consecutive meetings — March 23 to March 30 to April 2 to April 7 — before a budget finally passed 4-2. Now the city council is routing every add and cut request into a holding pen for resolution on May 12. Two elected bodies, two different buildings, the same governance rhythm. Everything is always deferred to the next meeting, and the next meeting is always two weeks away.

West submitting parking lot requests while absent — remove $250,000 from the tax buydown, seed a capital building reserve from unused fund balance — is the detail that tells Dana where one swing vote is leaning, and she files it for the May 12 prep call. Matthews is presumably somewhere in this room, and Dana knows exactly what he is going to say before he says it: school department, 62% of the budget, city in trouble. She has him on tape saying it across three different agenda items now. He is the season's most reliable narrator.

The segment she wants to produce between now and May 12 needs one of those 42 teachers on camera — preferably mid-career, South Portland-raised, coaching something — and a councilor willing to say "$26,000 per student, highest in the region" on the record. The combinatorial burden package she has been assembling since January — school referendum, $87 million public safety bond, 22% sewer rate increases — now has a city-side tax range to anchor it. The pieces are assembled. The June 9 referendum is the publishing deadline.
