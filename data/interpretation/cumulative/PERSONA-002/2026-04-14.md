# Cumulative Record: David (PERSONA-002)
## Meeting: City Council Regular Meeting -- April 14, 2026 -- 2026-04-14

### Interpretation

April 14 is the process milestone David has been tracking toward since February 10 — the school budget formally arriving before the governing body that controls the appropriation. And standing here with a spreadsheet he has been building since December, the meeting feels less like a revelation than a ceremony. Every figure in tonight's presentation — the $7.2M gap, the 78 position cuts, the 6% ceiling, the 23% elementary enrollment decline, the 82 positions added while 300 students left, the state's 20% actual funding against a 55% formula target — he has been carrying for months. The council is receiving this as a presentation. He has been living inside it since December 8.

What is genuinely new is the statutory framing. The city manager's position paper states plainly what David had inferred but never seen formally documented: under state law and the City Charter, the council may only move the total appropriation up or down. It cannot direct which positions survive, whether Kaler closes, or which programs get cut. Any bottom-line change sends the budget back to the board for revision before May 5. The structural boundary is right — operational decisions belong to the elected school board, not councilors. But it also clarifies the stakes: if the council reduces the appropriation, the board absorbs the cut in ways the council cannot control, with less than five weeks before the June 9 referendum.

That is the tension David is watching. The board built the budget to the 6% ceiling. The council's February 10 guidance was 3-6%, with most members gravitating toward 4-6%. Whether that gap surfaces as a formal appropriation reduction at the May 5 hearing is now the operative question. The fund balance is at zero — or below zero in some accounts, as April 7 confirmed. There is no cushion if any assumption comes in hot during execution. A council-imposed cut creates a second revision cycle in a calendar that was already tight before the one-week postponement from April 7 to April 14.

The irony is precise: the budget documents now posted to the district website are the primary source material David will pull before May 5. Whether they include year-over-year comparisons and a fund balance trend history determines whether the public voting on June 9 gets the full picture. That question has been open since December 1.
