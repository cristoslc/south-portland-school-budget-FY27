# Cumulative Record: Lila (PERSONA-014)
## Meeting: City Council Regular Meeting -- April 21, 2026 -- 2026-04-21

### Interpretation

My dad was gone for almost five hours last night and I fell asleep before he got home. He told me in the morning: Little League kids were there and they got certificates and a photo, and I kind of wish I could have seen that part because it sounds like the kind of meeting where kids are actually supposed to be in the room.

Then he said a school board member quit. Not the chair — the one who told the story about a school closing in 2005, before I was born, the one who had already been through this. Adrian Dowling. He resigned April 6, before the halt announcement, before the April 29 vote, and I didn't even know until last night. The people deciding what happens to my school are themselves leaving before it's decided. The chair cried at dinner two weeks ago, and now someone else just walked away entirely. The board has six people now, and it's going to stay six until November, which is after every vote that matters.

And June 9 is official. The council passed a formal order — same day as my last day of school, polls open 7 to 8 — and now the date has ink on it. I have been tracking June 9 since March 19, but "official" feels different than "we'll probably vote then." The countdown has a calendar square. The vote that determines the school budget, and whether my teacher survives, and whether the cuts stay cut, now has a door it cannot escape.

What I keep coming back to is that my dad was there for almost five hours and nobody talked about Dyer. Four hours went into something called the Comprehensive Plan — a document about where buildings can go for the next fifteen years — and two of my neighbors stood up and said they have cancer and live near the tank farms and their addresses are 47 Elmwood and 25 Ballard and Councilor West said monitors near the tanks found levels seven times higher than the state says is safe. Seven times. I have friends near the water. Nobody told them. The plan passed anyway, and the schools were not mentioned.

One lady from the Chamber of Commerce said the city needs more development because schools are "facing rising costs, difficult choices around staffing and facilities." That's the first time anyone from outside the school world has said out loud in a city meeting that something is wrong. But it was the reason to build more buildings, not the reason to fix what's breaking. Councilor Scott said the city needs to "build back our school populations." I know what that means now — fewer kids means fewer dollars means schools close — but nobody connected her words to the forty-two teachers or the nine scenarios or the vote in thirty-nine days. The adults are making a plan for fifteen years from now while my teacher might not have a job in ten weeks.

The Little League kids got a certificate. My school got silence. Five months, and the pattern has never broken.

### Emotional Register

Lila holds this meeting as the night two things collided that she didn't expect to hold together: a school board member she had been tracking as a fixed, permanent presence in the room where her future is decided simply stopped being there, and two neighbors with addresses she could walk to said they have cancer from the air near the water — and in between those two pieces of knowledge, five hours of city planning passed without anyone saying Dyer's name.
