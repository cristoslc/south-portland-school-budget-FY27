# Cumulative Record: Amira (PERSONA-013)
## Meeting: School Board Regular Meeting -- April 7, 2026 -- 2026-04-07

### Interpretation

The budget passed. Four to two. The percussion cut is still in it.

I've been carrying this specific worry since December — whether band would survive, whether the percussion ed tech who keeps the whole program running for five hundred kids in grades five through twelve would still be there in September. I watched parents come to meetings. I watched Matthew Emory say exactly the right thing at the microphone in March. I watched the board fail to vote, twice. And now the budget is moving to city council with the percussion position still cut, and computer science gone, and two PE teachers gone, and one of the two STEM teachers gone at the middle school. Mrs. Fletcher laid all of that out at the public comment microphone. Four board members voted yes anyway.

The thing I keep returning to is Jamie Watson's testimony. Five teachers at my school spent an entire school day not knowing if they had a job — because the administration posted a slide that said "related arts teacher" and didn't name anyone. Two of those teachers had already started applying to positions in other districts. While they were still teaching their students. While parent-teacher conferences were happening in the same building.

I found out about my ELA teacher leaving from a classmate in the hallway. I've always assumed that was because adults just didn't think to tell students. Watson's testimony shows the same gap on the other side: the administration left the teachers themselves in the dark, and some of them are already half-gone before anyone says a name out loud. That's not only a student problem. That's the system not telling the people inside it what's happening to them.

There was a student named Cass who came to the microphone — no board seat, no invitation, just a kid from Small School — and said it wasn't fair that teachers and kids had to be in this pressure. That's the fifth time I've encountered a voice my age across all these months. Jules reading a poem. The ten-year-old's question someone else had to carry to the room. Davidson sent home with his backpack before the vote. Matthew asking who would teach the drums. Now Cass, with no formal role, saying: this isn't fair. I know how to do that. I could do that.

Board member Richardson said middle school is a dark time and these related arts teachers are the joy and the light. That is the most accurate description of what it's actually like to be thirteen in a school that's losing things. She voted no. She was outvoted.

DeAngelis voted yes. In December she told the story of students who weren't listened to, and she seemed to understand what that does to kids. I'm not saying she doesn't care — I don't know what she's weighing. Maybe she believes there are no good options left. But she voted yes on a budget cutting the programs teachers are describing as the reason many middle schoolers feel they belong at school, and I keep trying to make those two things fit together and can't.

There might be one unnamed arts position back for one year if the state money comes through. Nobody said which one.
