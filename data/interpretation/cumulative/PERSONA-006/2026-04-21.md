# Cumulative Record: Tom (PERSONA-006)
## Meeting: 2026-04-21-city-council -- 2026-04-21

### Interpretation

Five months into this crisis, Tom finally has a referendum date: June 9. That's the one concrete thing the April 21 meeting produced, and the council set it in the first ten minutes and then moved on. Nearly five hours later, after a waterfront debate that filled the high school auditorium, Tom drove home still not knowing what a yes or no vote on June 9 actually costs him on a home assessed at $350,000. Nobody said that number out loud. The whole winter in one night.

The Eastern Waterfront conversation is where Tom has been since December — waiting for someone to show him that new development will actually relieve the pressure on homeowners. The Portland Regional Chamber rep finally said it in plain terms: without growing the tax base, costs don't disappear, they shift onto existing residents. Tom has been saying that since the TIF vote in December. But then the economist ran the numbers, and the honest answer is that Eastern Waterfront development nets roughly $2.5 million annually after infrastructure costs — less than 2% of the city budget. The Suncor tank farm pitch from January was supposed to generate $4 million. The Ocean Street TIF returns half its revenue to the developer for thirty years. The development relief valve Tom has been told to wait for keeps not materializing at a scale that closes a $7.2 million school gap by June.

The Comprehensive Plan passed 5-2, and Tom does not object to long-range planning. What he objects to is the planning director confirming what he already suspected: development revenue is years away after zoning, review, and construction. The council just adopted a fifteen-year guidance document while voters are six weeks from a referendum on a budget with 78 position cuts. Those two timelines are not in the same conversation.

The school board seat vacancy troubles him quietly. Adrian Dowling resigned April 6. The seat sits empty until November. A board that spent five months struggling to pass a budget with seven members is now managing a crisis with six, and the council accepted the vacancy without apparent concern.

The sewer rate number — 22% — Tom has been carrying since March 19, when the three-year schedule was confirmed. What April 21 adds is a public commenter saying it out loud at the council microphone, alongside Broadway congestion and $100 million in sea level rise costs. The stacking is no longer something Tom is calculating alone at his kitchen table. Other people in that room are doing the same math.
