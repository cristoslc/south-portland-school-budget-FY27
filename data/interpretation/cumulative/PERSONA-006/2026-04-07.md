# Cumulative Record: Tom (PERSONA-006)
## Meeting: School Board Regular Meeting -- April 7, 2026 -- 2026-04-07

### Interpretation

The budget passed. Four to two. Under the 6% ceiling. That's the headline, and Tom will take it — it's the one thing the city council asked for in February and the one thing the board delivered after five months of near-misses and a failed vote one week prior. But it arrived wrapped in everything else, and the everything else is the part he can't shake.

The health insurance savings are real money. An 11.5% placeholder came in at 5.14%, freeing up $655,000 the district didn't expect to have. In any other winter, Tom would call that a win and go home satisfied. Tuesday night, the money was gone before the meeting ended — redistributed into restored positions and contingency funds. That's been the pattern all winter: every dollar found gets spent before it can be held.

What stopped him cold was Meredith Diamond at the microphone with the FY25 audit. $2.37 million spent without board authorization. $400,000 in grant money forfeited because someone missed the reimbursement deadlines. $123,000 written back to the state for overstated grants. $600,000 in employee benefits under-withheld and absorbed. Tom waited for someone at the administration table to push back on any of those numbers. Nobody did. This is the organization that is now projecting a FY26 deficit — the current school year, not next — and the backstop is no longer even its own reserve. The backstop is the city's undesignated fund balance, the same reserve that the auditor grew to $5.8 million and that the council drew down twice in back-to-back weeks in March. The district cannot finish its own fiscal year in the black and has nothing left to cover the shortfall itself.

Board Member Richardson answered the administrator question Tom has been asking since December: no real cuts from the director roles. Shifted responsibilities, same structure. The support staff union president said this at March 23. Now it is confirmed from inside the board's own majority coalition, on the record.

The MaineCare gap surprised him. South Portland bills $70,000. Portland bills $600,000. Rockland, smaller, bills $500,000. The assistant superintendent called it worth exploring. If it is worth exploring now, the question of why it went unexplored while the fund balance was being spent down is one Tom does not expect anyone to answer.

John O'Connor's math — taxes from $4,200 to over $10,000 in five years — is a version of the calculation Tom does at his own kitchen table. He recognized it immediately.
