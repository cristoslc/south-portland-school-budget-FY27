# Cumulative Record: Jess (PERSONA-003)
## Meeting: City Council Budget Workshop #1 -- April 14, 2026 -- 2026-04-14

### Interpretation

The day after watching the board announce the reconfiguration delay, Jess watches the city formally take up the school budget for the first time. It was supposed to happen on April 7 — the public hearing that was already on the calendar when she learned about the March 18 layoff notices. But the school board hadn't finished its budget by then. The vote happened on the evening of April 7, barely in time, 4-2, and the city council gets its first workshop a week later.

What April 14 adds to her picture isn't the numbers. She knows every number. Forty-two teachers. Seventy-eight positions. Twenty-three percent enrollment drop in four years. Zero fund balance remaining. State paying 20 cents on the 55-cent dollar it owes. Jess has been living with these figures since December. Watching them presented formally to the city council isn't revelation — it's a milestone on the process calendar she's been tracking.

What's new is the legal architecture. The city manager's position paper makes it explicit: under state law and the City Charter, the council can only vote on the school budget's bottom-line total. It cannot tell the district to keep the behavioral strategist. It cannot protect Skillin. It cannot direct which of the 42 teachers stays and which goes. One number, up or down.

Four and a half months of watching city councilors receive school updates in passing comments and return to other business. Not one question about which teachers, which programs, which families. Jess documented every meeting and called it disconnection, disinterest, a pattern of civic abdication. That reading isn't wrong. But it isn't complete either. The council was also receiving updates on a budget it legally cannot direct. They were never going to do what she wanted. The law didn't give them that tool.

This lands with a specific flatness. Not anger — anger requires a belief that the outcome could have been different. It's more like the feeling of understanding, finally, why the room kept behaving the way it did, and not finding the explanation satisfying.

The June 9 referendum is what she has left. It's been on the calendar since March 19. It is the one point in the entire process where a vote from Jess — specifically from her, as a resident and not-yet-school-parent — registers. She can say yes or no on the bottom line. She cannot tell them which door her daughter walks through. But she can vote. That's something, and she intends to.
