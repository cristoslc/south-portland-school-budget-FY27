# Cumulative Record: Jess (PERSONA-003)
## Meeting: City Council Regular Meeting -- April 21, 2026 -- 2026-04-21

### Interpretation

Five minutes into a four-hour meeting about oil tanks and condos and six-story buildings near Bug Light, the city clerk announced that Adrian Dowling — school board vice chair, one of the four votes that passed Option A on March 30 — resigned effective April 6. The council's response: put it on the November ballot. Seven months vacant. I've watched this board make every structural decision that will shape Mia's first years of school, and they're about to do it one seat short, with no timeline for filling it, while 42 teachers are working out their last weeks.

Then I learned — from the consent calendar, in passing — that June 9 is a school budget validation referendum. Residents vote. A direct ballot on the whole thing. I've been tracking this process since December and nobody explained this existed until it appeared as a scheduling order. What happens if it fails? I don't know. The council moved on.

The rest of the evening was the comprehensive plan and the eastern waterfront, which is mostly not my world yet. But a Chamber representative stood up and said the school cuts are happening because the tax base isn't big enough, and new development is the answer. I've been watching the housing-enrollment coordination gap since December 1 — growth plans and school contraction running on separate tracks — and this is the first time anyone has stated the connection explicitly from the growth side. The problem is that the city planner laid out the timeline right after: comp plan adoption, then zoning, then planning board, then two ordinance readings, then development review, then construction. "Two to three years beyond the point of approval" before tax revenue materializes. The cuts happening now will be years old before any new development helps pay for what's been lost.

Councilor Scott said she hopes South Portland can "build back our school populations" by welcoming new residents. That's the first time I've heard a councilor name enrollment recovery as a goal, not just a fiscal problem. It landed. I'm not sure it means anything yet without a plan behind it, but it's different from what came before.

And then, almost as an aside during a discussion about petroleum tank emissions near Hill Street, Councilor Pride said the fumes aren't good for Kaylor School "while it's still open." Nobody followed up. Kaylor is the school the board voted to close on March 30. I looked it up after. It's a real building with a real address. The councilor knows it's closing. They just said it out loud and kept moving.

The June 9 referendum is what I'm holding onto. It's the one moment where my vote counts in this process. I intend to be there.
