# Cumulative Record: Ben (PERSONA-010)
## Meeting: City Council Budget Workshop #1 -- April 14, 2026 -- 2026-04-14

### Interpretation

Ben has been covering this budget crisis for four months, and April 14 is the meeting where the institutional mechanics finally snap into plain view — not through a revelation from a parent at a mic, but through a city manager's position paper that most people in that room probably skimmed.

The headline coming in was that the board barely made the April 7 deadline. They voted 4-2 that same night — the night originally scheduled as the Council public hearing — which meant the hearing had to shift to the April 14 workshop. That's not a scandal, but it's a detail. When you're eight weeks from a referendum and voters don't yet know what they're being asked to ratify, a week's delay in a process already running behind matters. Ben is not attributing bad faith to it. He is noting that the runway is short.

The position paper is the thing he will be thinking about when he sits down to write his pre-referendum piece. State law and the City Charter restrict the Council to one lever: the total bottom-line number. They cannot tell the district to save a teacher, restore a program, or reverse the Kaylor closure. That authority is entirely at the school board level. Ben has been covering two bodies all season as though they share accountability for the specifics. The mechanics say otherwise. Every parent who showed up at City Hall asking councilors to protect a program was asking the wrong body. The board sets the specifics. The Council sets the ceiling. That's the paragraph that will make his referendum piece useful to voters who have been getting fragments.

The workshop format adds a practical dimension. The school budget — 61 percent of the property tax bill, $7-8 million in structural cuts, 78 positions, a school closure — shared the night with the City Clerk, Human Resources, Social Services, Police, Fire, Library, Sustainability, Public Works, Facilities, and Parks and Rec. Smaller departments got five to ten minutes; larger ones fifteen. The depth of public scrutiny those specific cuts received in that room is limited. The real accountability moments happened at school board meetings most of Ben's readers never watched. That's the gap The Forecaster exists to fill, and he has eight weeks to do it.
