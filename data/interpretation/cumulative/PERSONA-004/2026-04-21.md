# Cumulative Record: Marcus (PERSONA-004)
## Meeting: 2026-04-21-city-council -- 2026-04-21

### Interpretation

I went. The meeting was at the high school, which has its own particular bitterness — the building where my colleagues are losing their positions hosted five hours of debate about condos near Bug Light Park. And in the middle of all of it, on the consent calendar, five seconds: June 9. The school budget referendum date, set and approved alongside a Little League proclamation. No discussion. The vote that decides whether 32 colleagues on a recall list ever come back just sailed through. That's the pattern I've been tracking since December 1, compressed into a consent calendar item.

The Dowling vacancy is the thing I'm still processing. Adrian Dowling resigned from District 5, and the council voted to leave that seat empty until November rather than fill it by appointment. Seven months. We're heading into the most consequential budget year in this district's history with a vacant school board seat, and the election that fills it happens on the same November ballot as three council seats and the public safety bond. The November election is now the governance moment that shapes the next budget cycle — who sits on the school board, who sits on the council. The union cannot treat November as background noise.

The comprehensive plan passed, and I heard the development argument. I've been tracking the city's parallel fiscal agenda since January 13, when the Mahoney bond first competed with the school referendum for voter goodwill. Now the Chamber of Commerce is using teacher job losses as a reason to approve their zoning. An advocacy director stood at the microphone and said South Portland's school staffing crisis is exactly why the city needs more development. He used us as the argument. A resident economist answered him with actual math: net annual revenue from eastern waterfront development, about $2.5 million, years from now, if everything goes right. Less than two percent of the city budget. The comp plan may be good policy for 2038. It does not help the 32 people on the recall list before June 9.

The alternative revenue ideas — nonprofit property taxation, other tools beyond property tax — floated and landed without a response. The council noted them and moved on. At this point, that's the pattern I expect.
