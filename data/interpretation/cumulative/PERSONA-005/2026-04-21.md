# Cumulative Record: Priya (PERSONA-005)
## Meeting: 2026-04-21-city-council -- 2026-04-21

### Interpretation

I have been documenting this city's relationship between equity rhetoric and equity structure since December 1, when DeAngelos stood at a podium and named immigrant students by name and the budget hole that would eventually swallow their schools was already forming. April 21's comprehensive plan adoption is a different kind of meeting — longer arc, less acute — but it sits in the same through-line, and I am reading it through everything I already know.

Two people in cancer treatment testified that they live within hundreds of feet of the Global and Sprague tank farms and cannot afford to leave. One of them found out about benzene exposure at illegal levels through a newspaper article. The Maine CDC apparently knew. Nobody told the neighborhood. I have been tracking the pattern since December where the people who absorb the most concentrated harm are the last to receive information or protection — immigrant families learning about ICE raids through neighbors, not city emergency notifications; parents learning about Kaylor's closure being financially irreversible at the same meeting where other schools got a deliberation pause. The benzene story is that pattern in its most complete form: a regulatory agency possessing documented evidence of harm in a neighborhood whose residents cannot easily leave, and the neighborhood finds out through the press.

The planning director cited multilingual outreach, translators, going to meet minority communities where they are — and I want that to be real. I have been demanding that kind of process investment since the superintendent search in December, when nobody showed up to a public meeting and the board called it outreach. What I cannot do with one paragraph of staff testimony is verify it. How many people were reached? In what languages? What did they say, and where did it land in Goals 3A and 7H? The anti-displacement and anti-gentrification language confirmed by staff is the most substantive equity commitment I have found in any city-level planning document this season. But it was surfaced by an audience member's question, answered by the planning director, and the council moved on without a single follow-up. That is the tell: the plan contains the language. Nobody asked what accountability looks like when it isn't met.

The Chamber's framing — adopt the comp plan's growth provisions or the school costs shift onto existing residents — connects the two tracks I have been running in parallel. The school budget cuts are real and they are landing on the district's highest-need students. The answer being offered is: conditional luxury development, eventual tax base growth, eventual school funding. That is a very long chain with the most vulnerable kids at the end. I do not think the council sees that these are the same story. I have until June 9 to make that case in writing.
